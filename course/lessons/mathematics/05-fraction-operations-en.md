# Operations with fractions: name the counting unit first

_Lead (summary):_ **We add, subtract, multiply, and divide fractions through meanings of operations rather than four unrelated rules.**

## Where we are on the map

After recognizing and comparing fractions, we calculate with them.

![Turning 1/2 + 1/3 into 5/6 by counting sixths](/static/course/mathematics/map-05-operations-en.svg)

## Begin with a familiar image

Half an apple and one third of an apple cannot be counted simply as “two parts” because the part sizes differ.

## Precise meaning

Addition and subtraction need a common counting unit. Multiplication takes a part of a number; division asks how many portions of a given size fit.

## The lesson's support signal

`meaning → model → calculation → check → explain in your own words`

name the unit → choose a model → calculate → check by inverse or estimate

## Worked example

`1/2 + 1/3 = 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6`. The answer is below 1 and greater than either addend.

## Example with a fading prompt

Change the numbers in the example and repeat the solution without copying its steps. Estimate first, calculate exactly, and check against the original condition.

## Recall without a prompt

1. Define the main idea in one sentence.
2. Rebuild the support signal from memory.
3. Solve the example with different numbers and explain why every step is valid.

## Find and correct the mistake

Writing `1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5` adds tops and bottoms separately but never creates a common counting unit.

## Transfer to a new setting

Apply the same relationship to something you can measure yourself. Name the quantities, units, and the boundary within which the model is valid. Check the answer by a second representation or an inverse operation.

## Exercise

**Required.** A workshop has `7/8` of a sheet and uses `3/16` per item. Find the number of complete items and the material left.

**With your own data.** Create one everyday example with the same structure and show it in words, a formula, and a check.

**Optional.** Seven days later, change the numbers and repeat without the support map.

## Open perspective

A relationship between two changing quantities leads to the idea of ratio.
