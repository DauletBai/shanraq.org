## Situation and question

A notebook says: “10 g of salt was added to 90 g of water and dissolved completely. Another 100 g of water was added. The solution is clear, so pure. Concentration is 5 mol/L.” Which claims follow from the data and which need correction? Analyse without an experiment.

## New terms, fully explained

A **component balance** compares its mass or amount before and after a change. **Sufficient data** means knowing every input needed for the chosen quantity. Mass fraction needs masses; molar concentration needs amount and final volume. Appearance alone establishes neither purity nor drinking safety.

## Predict first

Predict how salt fraction changes when water is added without losses. Can a mass of 200 g automatically imply 0.200 L? List every extra assumption such a conversion would require before calculating.

## Investigate or analyse given data

Write two rows: initially 10 g of salt, 90 g of water, 100 g total; finally 10 g of salt, 190 g of water, 200 g total. Additional supplied data give final volume of the second solution as 0.195 L and M(NaCl)=58.5 g/mol. These values are supplied, not measurements you made.

| Object / step | Given / calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 10 g + 90 g | 10 / 100 | 10% |
| + 100 g H₂O | 10 / 200 | 5% |
| V = 0.195 L | (10 / 58.5) / 0.195 | ≈ 0.877 mol/L |

## Worked example

Initially w=10/100=10%; after dilution w=10/200=5%. With the additional volume, n=10/58.5≈0.171 mol and c≈0.171/0.195≈0.877 mol/L. Without V, c cannot be determined. A mass percentage and mol/L describe different quantities.

## A common mistake

**Mistake:** inferring purity from clarity, replacing litres with kilograms, or ignoring solid residue. For another imaginary X with S=30 g per 100 g of water at 20 °C, adding 20 g to 50 g of water leaves 5 g solid at equilibrium; the liquid requires its own balance.

## Transfer to a new case

Final task: dissolve 12 g of sugar in 188 g of water. Take 50 g of homogeneous solution and add 25 g of water without losses. Find sugar mass in the portion and final mass fraction. Can molar concentration be found without final volume? Explain each step.

## Answer and recall

**Check:** initial fraction is 6%, the portion contains 3 g of sugar, and dilution gives 3/75=4%. Molar concentration needs volume. Assess your explanation, balance, units and limits; revisit lesson 53, 54, 56 or 57 when a related error remains.

## Exercise

Why cannot 5% be replaced by 5 mol/L? What extra data would allow conversion?

## Answers

Different quantities; molar mass and solution volume, or density together with solution mass, are needed.
