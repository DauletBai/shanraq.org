## Situation and question

In lesson one salt seemed to 'disappear'. We now need a model that explains both clear water and the salt's continued presence.

## New terms, fully explained

The **solvent** is the component in which another substance is distributed; here, water. The **solute** is the dissolved substance; here, salt. A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of solvent and solute. A **particle** in this chapter is a tiny symbolic unit in a model; we will identify the actual kinds later. A **model** explains and predicts; it is not a photograph. When table salt dissolves, its crystal separates into ions surrounded by water molecules; for now we draw simplified spheres. **Melting** is a solid turning liquid when conditions change, not mixing with a solvent.

An **atom** is a small unit of a chemical element; elements and atomic structure come later. A **molecule** is a group of bonded atoms; water consists of molecules. An **ion** is an electrically charged particle; table salt contains positive and negative ions. When salt dissolves, those ions spread through water. Spheres in the image are symbols: actual sizes, distances and movement are not shown.

## Predict first

Before the diagram, predict: if one or two small measured spoonfuls of salt are stirred into equal glasses of room-temperature water, does the salt's mass vanish? Can you keep adding salt without limit to one fixed volume of water? Do not test by taste.

## Investigate or analyse given data

Use two clear glasses with the same amount of water. With an adult, put a small measured portion of table salt in one; leave the other as a comparison. Draw visible grains before stirring and symbolic dots afterward. Label what you actually saw and what the model adds. Without materials, use these given observations: a small portion has been stirred in; water remains clear and grains cannot be seen.

## Worked example

Worked example: add 5 g of salt to 100 g of water. If none is lost during transfer, the mass of the **contents** after dissolving is 105 g; count the vessel separately. Here, mass of solution = mass of water + mass of salt added. The mass of the entire closed system stays the same before and after dissolving. Volumes need not add in the same way. In an open vessel, escaping water vapour must also be counted.

## A common mistake

**Mistake:** 'The salt melted.' Melting usually describes ice or another solid becoming liquid; salt **dissolved** in water. Drawing the particles the size of rice grains is another mistake: the diagram is enlarged and shows relationships, not actual size or colour.

## Transfer to a new case

Why does a paper filter that holds back sand fail to hold dissolved salt? Explain at the visible and model levels. What observation might challenge your model?

## Answer and recall

**Check:** water and dissolved components pass through an ordinary paper filter; large sand grains remain. In the model, small solution particles pass through pores. Pore and particle sizes are not to scale. From memory draw crystal + water → distribution → homogeneous solution; revisit tomorrow and in a week.
