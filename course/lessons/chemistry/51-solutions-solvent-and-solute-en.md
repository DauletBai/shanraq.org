## Situation and question

Three glasses contain pure water, water with salt completely dissolved, and water mixed with sand. Appearance alone cannot always establish composition. Recall the first salt investigation: invisible grains do not imply vanished matter. Build an explanation that does not require tasting samples.

## New terms, fully explained

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture whose components are distributed at molecular or ionic scale. The **solvent** provides the medium; a **solute** is dissolved in it. Here water is the solvent. A **suspension** is heterogeneous and contains relatively large dispersed particles. Solutions need not be aqueous or liquid.

## Predict first

Predict whether ordinary filter paper retains sand particles or dissolved salt. Must a clear filtrate be pure drinking water? Write separate claims about particle separation and drinking safety.

## Investigate or analyse given data

Analyse supplied observations: some sand settled and was caught by the filter, whereas salt solution passed through. Ordinary filtration removes suitable suspended particles but not dissolved ions. In the school model a well-mixed solution has the same composition in different portions if conditions remain unchanged.

## Worked example

10 g of salt completely dissolves in 90 g of water without losses. Solution mass is 100 g. A 20 g portion contains 2 g of salt because it is one fifth of the homogeneous solution. This follows from supplied composition, not visual measurement. It does not determine the volume change on mixing.

## A common mistake

**Mistake:** assuming every clear sample is a pure substance, or adding volumes as if they were masses. A clear solution remains a mixture. Component masses add in a mass balance; exact final solution volume requires measurement or density information.

## Transfer to a new case

Transfer: 6 g of sugar completely dissolves in 54 g of water. Identify the components, total mass and sugar mass in a 15 g portion. Explain why uniform composition is required. Use supplied data; no home observation is necessary.

## Answer and recall

**Check:** water is solvent, sugar is solute, total mass is 60 g, and one quarter contains 1.5 g of sugar. Dissolved contaminants may remain in the filtrate. Reconstruct “appearance → uniformity → composition → separation method”, without claiming that the water is safe to drink.

## Exercise

A solution contains 10 g of salt per 100 g of solution. How much is in 30 g? Does paper filtration remove it?

## Answers

3 g; ordinary filter paper does not remove dissolved salt.
