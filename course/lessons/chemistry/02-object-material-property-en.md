## Situation and question

Ask 'What is this drinking glass made of?' and hear 'glass'. Ask 'What is it like?' and hear 'transparent'. One scene has produced three kinds of words.

## New terms, fully explained

An **object** is an individual thing with shape and purpose: a glass, spoon or paper filter. A **material** is what an object is made from: glass, metal or paper. A **substance** is a kind of matter with a defined composition and properties; later we will distinguish individual substances from mixtures. Ordinary glass is usually a material containing several substances, not one pure substance. A **property** is a feature testable under stated conditions: transparency, colour or solubility. 'Transparent' is a property; 'glass' is a material.

**Matter** is anything that occupies space and has mass. **Composition** tells which substances are present and how much of each. **Solubility** describes whether a substance can distribute in a chosen solvent under stated conditions; different substances behave differently. One property alone cannot identify a material.

## Predict first

Before reading the example, classify these: steel spoon; steel; shine; water in a glass. Why is 'water' not the name of an object?

## Investigate or analyse given data

Make a card for one safe object: its name, material, two observable properties and how to check them by sight or by touching a dry outside surface. You may write that glass is fragile, but do not break it. Never test a liquid by taste or smell.

## Worked example

The spoon is an object, steel is its material, and its surface shining in the light is an observation. 'Shine' names a visible property. A plastic spoon remains a spoon while the material changes, and it may still shine. That is why the three layers matter.

## A common mistake

**Mistake:** treating glass as one pure substance with a single formula. Ordinary glass has several components. Another mistake is saying 'shiny, so it must be metal'; other materials can shine too.

## Transfer to a new case

For a clear plastic bottle, name the object, material and property. Why cannot transparency alone prove that it is glass? What further safe check would help?

## Answer and recall

**Check:** bottle is the object, plastic the material and transparency the property. Glass and some plastics are transparent; one feature cannot distinguish them. Draw 'object → material → property' from memory and explain a new example. Try again on an unfamiliar object in a week.
