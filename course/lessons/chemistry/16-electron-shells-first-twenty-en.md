## Situation and question

The cards for Ne and Na contain 10 and 11 electrons. Why does adding just one electron accompany a new period? Recall when electron count equals Z. If you leave out the condition that the atom is neutral, applying the next rule to an ion will give a wrong result. Write the conditions before you start.

## New terms, fully explained

An **electron shell** is a simplified description of electron populations in energy levels. The **ground state** is an atom’s lowest-energy state. For neutral atoms of the first 20 elements we use shell populations: H — 1; He — 2; Ne — 2,8; Na — 2,8,1; Ar — 2,8,8; K — 2,8,8,1; Ca — 2,8,8,2. Commas separate shells rather than making a decimal number. Electrons do not travel along these written sequences like planets on tracks.

## Predict first

Predict the arrangements for Mg with Z=12 and Cl with Z=17. Check that the numbers add to Z, not to the approximate atomic mass. Then predict how many occupied shells Ca has. State the scope: neutral atoms, ground state and the first 20 elements. Without those conditions, the claim becomes too broad.

## Investigate or analyse given data

Draw four columns for shells 1–4. Distribute paper marks for O, Mg, Cl and Ca using the reference data. Each row must contain Z marks in total. Do not connect electrical devices or investigate unknown materials. This is a counting model on paper, not an experiment for detecting electrons or observing their paths.

## Worked example

For Mg, 2+8+2=12 gives 2,8,2. For Cl, 2+8+7=17 gives 2,8,7. Both have three occupied shells and are in period 3. Ca has four: 2+8+8+2=20, so it is in period 4. Mg and Cl have different outer-shell populations despite sharing a period. For the neutral atoms studied here, period agrees with the number of occupied shells.

## A common mistake

**Mistake:** claiming that the third shell can never contain more than eight electrons. Its full capacity is larger; filling beyond Ca needs a more detailed subshell model. Our lists do not cover every element or excited state. Drawn shell spacing, dot sizes and individual dot positions are symbolic, not measured atomic geometry.

## Transfer to a new case

One card gives Z=13 and 2,8,3. Identify its element, period and outer-electron count. Another card with the same Z has only 10 electrons. Can you keep the neutral atom’s arrangement unchanged? Do not look for a new element: return to the proton and electron counts and calculate the charge first.

## Answer and recall

**Check:** Z=13 identifies Al in period 3, with three outer electrons in the neutral atom. The particle with 10 electrons has charge +3 and arrangement 2,8 in this model. Its nucleus is still aluminium’s; lesson 21 examines ions’ table positions. Explain both the model’s usefulness and its limit to the first 20 neutral ground-state atoms without looking at the reference.

## Exercise

1. Give Ca’s shell arrangement. How do you check the sum?
2. Is eight the third shell’s full capacity?

## Answers

2,8,8,2; total 20=Z; no, it is not the shell’s full capacity.
