## Situation and question

The notebook says: “The indicator is red, so hydrochloric acid has been found.” It gives neither the indicator’s name nor its scale. Is that sufficient? A sample’s own colour and an added indicator’s response are different observations. Use printed observation cards only; do not handle liquids or prepare indicators.

## New terms, fully explained

An **indicator** changes colour over a particular acidity range. A **transition interval** is a pH range, not one exact value. Our supplied card says litmus is red in acidic solution, purple in neutral solution and blue in basic solution. Interpret these signs together with the indicator name and observation conditions.

## Predict first

Predict whether blue litmus identifies NaOH specifically. Could colourless phenolphthalein indicate either an acidic or a near-neutral solution? List alternatives first, then use this reference: it is colourless below roughly pH 8.2, with its pink transition usually around 8.2–10.

## Worked example

Under the given scale, A is acidic, B neutral and C basic. D alone is insufficient: colourlessness is compatible with a broad range including acidic and neutral conditions. Another observation is needed. None of these results by itself names a particular acid or base.

## A common mistake

**Mistake:** treating any red colour as evidence of acidity without naming the indicator, or reading a colour scale as a precise digital instrument. Different indicators have different transitions. Universal-indicator colour charts vary with the product, so use the supplied chart. Do not identify unknown household liquids using this teaching table.
