# Why does a metal spoon feel colder than a wooden one?

_Лид (summary):_ **Compare materials at the same room temperature and trace energy transfer through contact.**

## A real-life question and prediction

## Words to understand

**Conduction** is energy transfer through a material as neighbouring parts interact, without the whole material travelling from end to end. A good thermal **conductor** passes energy relatively quickly; an **insulator** passes it more slowly under comparable conditions. Skin sensation depends on transfer rate, contact area and starting hand temperature. Metal and wood can have the same temperature yet remove energy from skin at different rates.

## A worked example

In a teaching diagram the left part is at 30 °C and the right at 20 °C. On contact, energy passes left to right. Those two temperatures alone do not yield a number of joules: material, mass and further conditions are needed. With no external energy supply the temperatures move towards each other. The drawing shows direction, not an exact promised final value.

## Test it safely

Compare room-temperature spoons by touch, then measure their temperatures with a suitable thermometer if available. Record material, time spent side by side and your sensation. Do not put the spoons in boiling water or press metal against frostbitten skin. Without instruments, keep your conclusion conditional: “feels colder” is not “measured colder”.

## An easy mistake to make

Mistake: drawing metal atoms flying from spoon into hand. Conduction transfers energy; the spoon material as a whole stays in place. Do not label everything cool to the touch as initially colder.
