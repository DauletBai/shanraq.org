# Why can a small cup of water warm faster than a large one?

_Лид (summary):_ **Connect mass, temperature change and transferred energy without reporting a calculation as an experiment.**

## A real-life question and prediction

A kitchen has a small and a large cup of water. If both receive the same energy, their temperatures may change differently. In a first model, more water means more particles share the energy. Equal heater power, however, does not guarantee equal losses. Predict the energy needed to warm twice as much water by the same 5 °C.

## Words to understand

**Mass** m describes how much material is in our model and is measured here in kilograms. **Specific heat capacity** c is the energy needed to raise 1 kg of a material by 1 °C when no phase change occurs; our school value for water is approximately 4200 J/(kg·°C). **Temperature change** is ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial. Hence the energy received is Q ≈ mcΔT. “Specific” here means “per unit mass”.

## A worked example

Teaching data: 0.2 kg of water warms from 20 to 25 °C. ΔT = 5 °C, so Q ≈ 0.2 × 4200 × 5 = 4200 J. For 0.4 kg with the same change, the result is 8400 J. That is energy reaching the water in a simplified model; a real appliance also warms its container and loses energy to the room.

## Test it safely

Without a heater, sketch two cups labelled 0.2 and 0.4 kg and compare the calculations. You may measure the starting temperature of room-temperature water at home; no stove or boiling water is required. Write units at every step: kg × J/(kg·°C) × °C = J.

## An easy mistake to make

Mistake: using 25 °C where the formula needs ΔT = 5 °C. It describes a change, not a final reading. Also do not claim that 4200 J was measured when c and mass were supplied teaching values.
