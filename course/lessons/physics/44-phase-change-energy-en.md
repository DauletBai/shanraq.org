# Where does energy go while ice melts?

_Лид (summary):_ **Temperature can remain nearly steady while energy arrives: the material changes state.**

## A real-life question and prediction

Ice in a cup slowly becomes water under ordinary conditions. You might expect every added bit of energy to raise a thermometer reading immediately, but that is not so in an idealized melting stage of pure ice at 0 °C. Energy changes interactions among particles. Predict whether the same calculation handles warming liquid water and melting ice of the same mass.

## Words to understand

**Melting** changes a solid to a liquid; the reverse is **freezing**. **Specific latent heat of fusion** L is the energy needed to melt 1 kg at its melting temperature; the school-model value for ice is about 334 kJ/kg. 1 kJ = 1000 J. At unchanged pressure, a pure substance ideally remains at one temperature during the phase change until it ends. **Evaporation** changes liquid to gas and has different conditions and an associated latent heat.

## A worked example

Teaching condition: 0.1 kg of ice already starts at 0 °C. To melt it completely there, Q = mL = 0.1 × 334 = 33.4 kJ. The resulting liquid is still near 0 °C just after melting. Warming it to 5 °C then takes a separate Q ≈ 0.1 × 4200 × 5 = 2100 J = 2.1 kJ. Add these stages only if you show both.

## Test it safely

If you have an ice cube, observe it melting on a dish at room temperature. Record how the amount of ice changes over time; do not report a measured temperature without a thermometer. Do not press frozen items to your tongue or use dry ice. Without an experiment, draw: ice at 0 °C → mixture → liquid at 0 °C → warmer liquid.

## An easy mistake to make

Mistake: applying Q = mcΔT only to the melting stage, setting ΔT = 0, and concluding “no energy needed”. State change requires energy. Also keep kJ and J distinct: 33.4 kJ is 33,400 J.
