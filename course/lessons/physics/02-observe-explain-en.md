# Observation, explanation, and honest records

_Лид (summary):_ **Separate what you saw from why you think it happened, and record inconvenient results too.**

## A question from daily life

You let go of a swing and it gradually stopped. You saw the stopping; the reason is still a proposal. Those are different statements.

## Words and meanings

A **pendulum** is a weight hung from a string so it can swing from side to side. An **observation** reports what you saw or measured: 'after ten swings the motion was smaller.' An **explanation** proposes a cause: 'air and the pivot resisted the motion.' **Data** are recorded observations and numbers. A **data source** says who made a record, when, and how. If you did not count ten swings, do not add that number to make a sentence look scientific.

## Predict before checking

Before watching again, predict whether the swing will travel less far from the middle after several trips. That distance from the middle to the farthest visible point is called its **extent** here; we will learn a precise measure later.

## Do it and record it

Ask an adult to gently move an empty safe swing, or watch a short unedited clip. Record only what you actually see, such as 'each farthest position moved closer to the middle.' If you cannot observe it, use the numbers below and label them 'worked example'.

## A worked example

Record A: 'The pendulum completed 10 swings in 11.0 seconds' is data if it was actually measured. Record B: 'It slowed because of friction' is an explanation to test separately. Record C: 'Worked data: 11.0 seconds' is an honest use of a supplied number.

## A common mistake

Do not call an invented number your experimental result. Do not treat the first plausible explanation as proven. 'I do not know yet' is a valid scientific statement when followed by a plan to check.

## A new situation

A phone became warm during a game. Name one observation and two possible causes that would need different checks. Do not dismantle or deliberately heat the phone.

## Notebook entry and self-check

Make three notebook columns: 'observed', 'I think', and 'how I would test'. Mark the source: your own observation or a worked example. Check that the first column does not claim an invisible cause.

## Slow the idea down

Imagine the record: “On the sixth return, the weight no longer reached the mark on the paper.” That is an observation: someone nearby could check the mark. “Air resistance stopped it” is an explanation. You cannot see the air acting, and friction at the support might also matter. Write down both possibilities and ask what observation could distinguish them. Until then, neither is a demonstrated cause.

Honest recording matters most when a result is inconvenient. If the weight touched the table during one repeat, note it beside the number; do not silently erase it. If you only read the table in this lesson, label it **worked data**, not your measurement. Record the date, method, and source in your notebook. No personal information is needed.

## Check with a new case

A puddle was large in the morning and smaller in the evening. “Its size changed” is an observation if you have comparable marks. “The sun evaporated the water” is one possible explanation. Name another cause and a safe way to test it.
