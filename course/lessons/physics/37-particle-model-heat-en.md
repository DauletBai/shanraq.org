# What happens to water when it warms up?

_Лид (summary):_ **Move from a drop of water to a model of invisible particles. The model helps explain heating, but its drawing is not a photograph.**

## A real-life question and prediction

Put two identical closed cups on a desk: one contains water warmer than room air, the other cooler. The first cools and the second warms towards room temperature. Which part can you see, and which part requires a model? Imagine water as a very large number of tiny particles. Do not draw each as a miniature cup. Predict whether the mass of a closed cup changes just because its temperature changes.

Pause before the explanation: what exactly do you expect to see, and why? Write a prediction before calculating. After the lesson, separate what you observed, what you calculated from supplied numbers, and what you only inferred about invisible particles. That record lets you correct a model without replacing evidence with a neat answer.

## Words to understand

A **particle** is a tiny constituent of matter; moving, interacting dots are enough for this school model. A **model** is a simplified picture tested against observations. **Thermal motion** is the irregular motion of particles. Heating neither creates new particles nor makes each one bigger. Later we will connect temperature to their average energy of motion; at this point it is an explanatory idea, not a particle path you measured.

## A worked example

In a teaching model, two closed cups each contain 200 g of water. After gentle warming, a cup still contains 200 g if nothing spills and vapour cannot escape. Different temperatures do not imply different numbers of particles in equally filled cups. Remove a lid and allow water to evaporate out: the mass of water left can decrease because the condition changed.

## Test it safely

Put one drop of food dye into each of two clear cups of room-temperature and slightly warm water. Boiling water is unnecessary. Record equal volumes and where and when the colour spreads. Stirring with a spoon introduces another process. If you have no materials, sketch two possible outcomes and state what observation would support each.

## An easy mistake to make

Mistake: seeing dye spread and claiming you saw individual molecules. A coloured patch is visible; single water particles are not. Water currents also affect mixing, so one observation cannot measure particle speed.
