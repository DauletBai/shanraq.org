# Why is a battery marked 3 V?

_Лид (summary):_ **Introduce voltage as energy transferred per unit charge, and measure it between two points.**

## Situation and prediction

A lamp may carry current, but without a source it would not continually receive energy. In our notebook the battery is like an energy pump: that comparison reminds us that the source does work, not that water flows in the wire. Predict whether the same source gives different voltage if only the length of an ideal wire changes.

## New words, step by step

**Voltage** U between two points is energy ΔE transferred per coulomb of charge Δq: U = ΔE/Δq. A **volt** (V) is a joule per coulomb: 1 V = 1 J/C. It is a difference between points; a single point needs a chosen reference. A **voltmeter** is connected in parallel across a component, to its two ends, without breaking the main path. A cell labelled 3 V can show a different terminal voltage while supplying current because it has internal resistance.

## Working through supplied data

In supplied teaching data a lamp receives 12 J when 4 C pass through it. U = 12/4 = 3 V across the lamp. This does not imply 3 A: amperes count charge per second, and no time was given. Voltage drop along an ideal wire is negligible in this school model; a long real wire may have measurable resistance.

## A safe check

Label both lamp terminals A and B on your diagram and write U_AB. With a ready-made kit and voltmeter, an adult selects the range and attaches probes across the low-voltage lamp. Never test a wall outlet or open a cell.

## A reasoning trap

A mistake is to treat volts as the number of electrons inside a cell. Voltage is energy per charge and belongs to a pair of points. Energy needs charge as well; power also needs time.
