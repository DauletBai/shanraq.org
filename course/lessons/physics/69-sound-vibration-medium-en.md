# Why does a ruler make sound, and does sound need air?

_Лид (summary):_ **Connect a vibrating source to pressure changes in air and see why sound does not cross a vacuum.**

## Question and prediction

Press a ruler against a table and bend its free end gently. While it vibrates you hear a sound; as motion dies away, so does the sound. You do not see a stream of air travelling from desk to ear, but neighbouring regions pass a pressure change along. Predict what changes if the free part of the ruler is shorter.

## New words without gaps

A **sound source** vibrates and disturbs a medium. A **sound wave** in air consists of alternating compression and rarefaction regions travelling away from the source. **Longitudinal** means that particles of the medium oscillate approximately along the direction of travel. **Amplitude** is the greatest displacement from rest; it relates to the size of the vibration, but perceived loudness also depends on distance and hearing. Sound cannot travel through empty space without a medium.

## Work through supplied data

In the teaching diagram, air markers remain near their mean positions while compressions pass to the right. This is qualitative: no sound speed can be calculated from it. Sound in ordinary air travels much faster than the ruler's visible motion, but the ruler need not travel to the listener.

## Test safely

Try this quietly without striking the table or putting anything near an ear. Keep the free length fixed and vary only the initial bend, then change length separately. Record what you hear as observation; label the compression explanation as a model.

## A common mistake

A loud sound does not prove that air particles fly all the way from source to ear. Nor can we claim that any ruler produces just one exact frequency: shape and clamping matter. The compression drawing leaves out details of real sounds.
