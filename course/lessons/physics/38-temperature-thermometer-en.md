# Can you measure warmth with your hand?

_Лид (summary):_ **A hand senses change, but temperature needs an instrument and a scale. Learn to read three results honestly.**

## A real-life question and prediction

After a cold walk, a room-temperature table feels warm; after a warm bath the same table feels cool. The table may not have changed. Skin responds to the direction of energy transfer and its own state; it is not a precise thermometer. Predict what a thermometer will show if you measure the same room air twice in succession.

## Words to understand

**Temperature** helps predict the direction of spontaneous energy transfer on thermal contact: from higher to lower temperature. The Celsius scale uses °C. **Scale interval** is the difference between adjacent marks. **Thermal equilibrium** is reached when contact no longer produces average energy transfer due to a temperature difference. A thermometer needs time to approach the temperature of its surroundings.

## A worked example

A teaching scale runs from 0 to 40 °C in 1 °C steps. Three supplied room readings are 21, 22, and 21 °C. Their mean is (21 + 22 + 21)/3 = 21.3 °C. A decimal is useful when comparing means, but it does not mean the instrument could resolve a tenth of a degree. For an individual reading, report the 1 °C interval and the measurement conditions.

## Test it safely

Use an ordinary room thermometer, not a mercury medical thermometer. Leave it in the room for several minutes, read it at eye level three times, and record the time. Do not warm the sensor with your hand. Without a thermometer, compare drawn scales marked every 1 and 2 °C: which permits a finer reading, and why?

## An easy mistake to make

Mistake: treating a sensation as a thermometer, or claiming that 21.3 °C is the precision of the real instrument. Averaging smooths changes but does not refine the scale interval. Nor can you compare different room positions without recording where the sensor was.
