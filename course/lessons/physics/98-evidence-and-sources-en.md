# What do the numbers prove, and what does a source prove?

_Лид (summary):_ **Separate supplied data, physics explanation and guesses.**

## Question and prediction

It is easy to write “Fabric always stops every cart better”. Six practice numbers have not earned “always”. It is just as easy to copy a definition of friction and call it a personal observation. A careful researcher labels where each important sentence came from.

## New words without gaps

Data are outcomes recorded under a stated procedure. A source is a text or record where someone else can check an explanation; cite the relevant page. A claim is a statement we try to support. A limitation is a condition beyond which evidence is insufficient. OpenStax explains contact forces and friction, but cannot verify the invented 82 and 45 cm in our exercise.

## Work through supplied data

Three lines: “means of 83 and 45 cm” is a calculation from supplied data; “a contact force can oppose motion” is a textbook explanation; “every cart loses exactly 38 cm” is unsupported generalisation. Distance is measured in cm; force in N. A textbook link does not turn a calculation into your own experiment.

## Test safely

Make a claim log: sentence; kind of support; exact table row or page link; limit. For outside text write the organisation, page title and address. If you did not run the trial, label its numbers “supplied for practice”, not “we measured”.

## A common mistake

Mistake: believing one citation supports every nearby sentence. Transfer: a friend says smooth floors are always more dangerous than carpets. What new conditions and observations would such a real-world conclusion need?
