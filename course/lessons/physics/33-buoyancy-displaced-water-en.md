# Where does an object’s weight “go” underwater?

_Лид (summary):_ **Discover buoyancy through displaced water and distinguish weight, a force-meter reading and floating.**

## Story and prediction

A small stone hanging from a string feels easier to hold underwater than in air. It has not lost mass; water pushes it upwards. Why upwards? Its lower face is deeper, so water pressure there is greater than at its upper face. Sideways forces cancel in a simple symmetric model. The resulting fluid force points upwards.

## Words to learn

**Displaced volume** is the volume of the object’s submerged part, which takes the place of liquid. **Buoyant force** Fᵦ is the fluid’s net upward force: Fᵦ = ρfluid g Vsubmerged. Here ρfluid is liquid density and Vsubmerged is submerged volume. Archimedes’ principle says this force equals the weight of displaced fluid for a stationary liquid. A **force meter** holding the object reads less by Fᵦ, but its mass does not change.

## Check the model with numbers

Worked cube: volume 0.0001 m³, completely underwater. Fᵦ = 1000 × 10 × 0.0001 = 1 N. If its weight in air is 3 N and it is held still underwater, a force meter would read about 2 N. If half the cube is submerged, displaced volume halves and Fᵦ ≈ 0.5 N in this model. This assumes the stated geometry and the same water density.

## A safe investigation

Put a light plastic cap in a shallow bowl of water and sketch how much of it lies below the surface. Do not attach the example’s numbers to your cap: you did not measure its volume. With no equipment, work another case: a fully submerged body displaces 0.0002 m³ of water; estimate Fᵦ = 2 N. Record that “easier to hold” is a sensation while 2 N is calculated.

## A likely mistake

Trap: buoyant force always equals the object’s whole weight. That holds for a freely floating body in equilibrium, but not for a cube held underwater or an object sinking.
