# Why can a magnet lift a clip but not every coin?

_Лид (summary):_ **Test what an object is made of rather than its colour or weight, and begin the magnetic page of the lab notebook.**

## Situation and prediction

A steel paper clip, aluminium foil and a plastic lid lie on a table. The magnet lifts the clip although the foil is shinier. Predict each result before reading on. If you do not know a coin's composition, do not promise attraction: different issues use different alloys.

## New words, step by step

A **magnet** produces a magnetic field. A **magnetic interaction** is an effect on another magnet or a suitable material without direct contact. Iron, some steels, nickel and cobalt can be strongly attracted; wood, plastic and aluminium do not behave that way in this ordinary test. **Material** means what an object is made of, not its shape or colour. A magnet does not attract every metal: aluminium is a metal too.

## Working through supplied data

Compare three items with the same 5 mm gap. Supplied example data: steel clip — noticeable attraction; aluminium strip — none noticed; plastic lid — none noticed. This is a qualitative observation, not a force measurement. If a coin is lifted, check its composition before making a claim about all coins.

## A safe check

Set three small objects on a table and approach each from the same side with an enclosed classroom magnet. Change only the object and keep the gap similar. Keep strong magnets away from electronics, medical implants and small children; do not dismantle devices for magnets. Without equipment, mark a filled table as predictions rather than results.

## Where the reasoning breaks

The claim 'magnets attract all metals' fails for aluminium foil. Another mistake is to picture a field as a visible fluid surrounding the magnet. A field is a model of an effect; next we will map its direction.
