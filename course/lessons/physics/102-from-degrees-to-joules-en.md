# Can a temperature drop be converted into joules?

_Лид (summary):_ **Connect water mass, specific heat capacity and temperature change without mistaking a model for a meter.**

## The question

The first investigation gave drops of 9 °C and 6 °C. It is tempting to say the open cup lost “three more joules”. But degrees and joules describe different quantities. We also need water mass and its specific heat capacity. Use the same supplied data so the calculation stays connected to the observation.

## Concept map

The mass of water tells us how many kilograms of water we used; its unit is the kilogram (kg). Specific heat capacity c tells us the energy needed to warm 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C. For liquid water, a school-level approximation is c = 4200 J/(kg·°C). A joule (J) is a unit of energy. With constant mass and no change of state, the magnitude of energy given up by the water is estimated by Q = m·c·ΔT. ΔT means the size of the temperature change, not a new measuring device.

## Worked numbers

Suppose each cup contains 0.20 kg of water. Open cup: Q = 0.20 × 4200 × 9 = 7560 J. Covered cup: Q = 0.20 × 4200 × 6 = 5040 J. The estimated difference is 2520 J. This estimates energy given up by the water in fifteen minutes under a simplified model. It is not automatically the loss from the whole system: the glass stores energy, water can evaporate, and the room and lid exchange energy. We did not measure evaporated mass either.

## Safe practice

Draw a support diagram: water → cup wall/air/vapour → surroundings. Label a plausible transfer route on each arrow. Check the units: kg × J/(kg·°C) × °C = J. Recalculate the covered cup for 0.10 kg of water and the same 6 °C drop: the result is 2520 J. That is a mathematical comparison, not a new measurement. Do not handle boiling water or use electrical weighing equipment beside water.

## Check your reasoning

Self-check: why can the 3 °C difference between cups alone not determine Q? When would a calculation using c for liquid water be insufficient? Transfer: if one cup holds twice as much water, does the same temperature drop mean the same energy loss?
