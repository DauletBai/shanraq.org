# How much energy does a tiny lamp use?

_Лид (summary):_ **Separate power, energy and cost so watts do not get confused with watt-hours.**

## Situation and prediction

Two devices run for different lengths of time. The more powerful one need not use more energy in a day: duration matters. Before calculating, predict which teaching lamp receives more energy: 0.06 W for 60 s or 0.03 W for 180 s. We are extending the notebook: we now estimate electrical energy use as carefully as we estimated room heating.

## New words, step by step

**Power** P is the rate of energy transfer, P = E/t. Its unit, the **watt** (W), is a joule per second. For a circuit component P = UI: each coulomb transfers U joules and I coulombs pass each second. **Energy** E = Pt = UIt is measured in joules. A **watt-hour** (Wh) is also energy: 1 Wh = 3600 J. A **kilowatt-hour** (kWh) = 1000 Wh is used on electricity bills; multiply kWh by a local tariff for cost, and tariffs depend on location and terms.

## Working through supplied data

Supplied data give 3 V, 0.02 A and 60 s for a lamp. P = 3 × 0.02 = 0.06 W. E = 0.06 × 60 = 3.6 J = 0.001 Wh. A second device at 0.03 W for 180 s receives 5.4 J despite lower power. These numbers do not prove that every LED operates at 3 V and 0.02 A.

## A safe check

Draw energy bars for the two supplied devices. If you have a household bill, find its kWh unit, but never alter wiring or open an electrical panel. The whole activity can be done on paper.

## A reasoning trap

A mistake is to give the minute's total as 0.06 W. Watts are a rate; the total is 3.6 J or 0.001 Wh. Energy received by a lamp is also not the quantity of charge stored by its battery.
