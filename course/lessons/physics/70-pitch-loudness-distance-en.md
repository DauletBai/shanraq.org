# High or loud: why are they different?

_Лид (summary):_ **Compare two sounds to separate frequency, amplitude and the weakening of sound with distance.**

## Question and prediction

A piano can play a high quiet note and a low loud note. 'High' describes perceived pitch; 'loud' describes the strength of the sound as heard. Predict first: if you move away from a speaker, does its number of vibrations per second change?

## New words without gaps

For a simple sound, **pitch** mainly follows frequency: more cycles per second usually means a higher pitch. **Loudness** depends on vibration amplitude and on how much sound energy reaches the ear; it is not another name for frequency. **Distance from the source** matters too. In open space a wave spreads its energy across a growing area, so sound normally weakens with distance. Reflections indoors complicate the picture.

## Work through supplied data

One supplied signal has frequency 200 Hz and small amplitude. A second has 400 Hz and the same amplitude. The second is generally perceived as higher, but the data do not say it is twice as loud. A third signal is 200 Hz with greater amplitude at the same listener: its pitch is about the same, while perceived strength changes. The diagram gives no calibrated decibel scale.

## Test safely

Use only a comfortable volume. Hum two notes quietly or compare prepared recordings at low level; do not turn the volume up for the experiment. Ask someone which note is higher without prompting them to say 'louder'. Never test close to a loud speaker.

## A common mistake

It is wrong to read '400 Hz' as 400 units of loudness. Hertz counts cycles, not strength of perception. Even a high sound can be quiet. Moving away from a stationary source usually leaves its frequency unchanged; what changes is delivery to the ear.
