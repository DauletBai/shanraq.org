# How does a lens make an image, and why does the screen matter?

_Лид (summary):_ **Separate object, rays and real image, and learn when a converging lens can form one.**

## Question and prediction

Place a magnifying glass between a desk lamp and white paper. At some distances the paper shows a bright, inverted image of the lamp. Never look at the Sun through a lens or focus sunlight onto paper: it can heat up. A paper ray diagram without a lamp is safer and sufficient here.

## New words without gaps

A **lens** is a transparent body with curved surfaces that redirects rays. A **converging lens** can bring parallel rays approximately to a **focus**. In the thin-lens model, the distance from its centre to the focus is **focal length f**. A **real image** forms where transmitted rays actually meet; a screen can catch it. If the object is inside the focal length, no real image forms on a screen behind the lens.

## Work through supplied data

Supplied example: f = 10 cm, and the object is 20 cm left of a thin lens. The equation 1/f = 1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ gives 1/10 = 1/20 + 1/dᵢ, hence dᵢ = 20 cm right. In the diagram, one ray runs parallel to the main axis then through the right focus; another passes the lens centre with negligible deflection. Their meeting below the axis gives an inverted image of equal height in the ideal model.

## Test safely

Draw on squared paper with a scale of one square to 5 cm and check the intersection. If using a small LED lamp and lens, keep them steady and avoid shining into eyes. A real lens has thickness and aberrations; the exact numerical result belongs to the teaching model.

## A common mistake

The image does not always form at the focus. That is where parallel rays from a distant source meet; an object at 20 cm with f = 10 cm forms its image at 20 cm. Also, not everything seen through a lens is a real image that paper can catch.
