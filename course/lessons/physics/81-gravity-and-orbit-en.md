# Why does an orbiting satellite not fall straight down?

_Лид (summary):_ **Join gravity to sideways speed and a curved path.**

## Question and prediction

Throw a soft ball horizontally: it moves forward and down. Imagine increasing its sideways speed while Earth's surface curves away beneath it. Why can a fast satellite keep 'falling' without reaching the ground? This is a thought model, not an invitation to launch objects.

## New words without gaps

Gravity is attraction between bodies with mass. An orbit is a path on which gravity continually changes the direction of motion. Sideways velocity points along the path; gravitational force points approximately towards Earth's centre. Acceleration can mean a change in speed or direction. A low orbit must be above dense air, otherwise drag slows the satellite. It is not free of Earth's gravity.

## Work through supplied data

In the supplied drawing at point A the velocity arrow points right and the force arrow points down towards Earth's centre. A short time later the direction of travel has turned. This does not mean the force points forward. A circular orbit just above an airless Earth would require roughly 7.9 km/s; actual orbits vary in height and shape. The number is not measured from the drawing and does not describe every satellite.

## Test safely

Draw Earth as a circle and mark point A. Use two colours for velocity tangent to the path and force towards the centre. Extend a short section of path and explain why a straight line differs from an orbit. Do not drop or throw objects from heights; the paper model is enough.

## A common mistake

Mistake: weightlessness means no gravity. In orbit a satellite and objects inside it fall together. New case: if gravity vanished instantly while sideways velocity remained, which way would the object travel in this model?
